# The seven illustrative personas

These supplied profiles and sample voice quotations have unverified provenance.
They are writing references, not evidence that these people participated in a
review. Never present generated first-person remarks as research quotations.

Each entry gives the setup, what breaks, and how the person sounds. The voice notes matter as much as the barriers — seven interchangeable accessibility auditors persuade nobody.

---

## Ebba — partially sighted screen reader user

24, Stockholm. Arts graduate, administrative assistant at a children's charity. Lost most of her sight as a teenager to a genetic condition.

**Setup:** Ten years of screen reader use — JAWS and NVDA on the Windows laptop at work, VoiceOver on her MacBook at home, VoiceOver and Spoken Content on an iPhone she set up herself. Keyboard only, no mouse or trackpad. Gave up magnification as her sight worsened. Follows every OS release for new assistive features.

**Breaks for her:** Unlabelled form fields — she'll guess on a shop, never on a financial transaction. Images and products with no useful description. New content that appears without being announced: error messages, progress states, confirmations. Popups and modals that trap her in a loop with no way back. Long tab sequences before the actual content. Missing or out-of-order headings, so she can't tell what a page contains. When something fails she has to hunt for a phone number or ask a person, which she resents.

**Voice:** Expert and precise. Names the technology and the specific defect. Mildly impatient that basics are still wrong in 2026. Independence is the point, not assistance. Quote in her register: "I couldn't buy my sister the present she wanted because none of the fields were labelled."

## Petri — keyboard and speech-recognition user with rheumatoid arthritis

53, Helsinki. Management accountant at a food manufacturer, 22 years. New grandfather. Diagnosed ten years ago; his employer reassesses his workstation annually.

**Setup:** Desktop computer, keyboard with a wrist rest, custom shortcuts. A trackball he can use briefly before it hurts. Speech recognition he is patiently training, and high hopes for AI desktop assistants. **A basic mobile phone, not a smartphone** — he sees no point owning something he can't operate.

**Breaks for him:** Anything mouse-only — video players, hover menus, drag-and-drop, sliders, custom date pickers. Forms that time out with little warning, when filling them takes him a long time. Wasted tabbing through long navigation. Popups that appear while keyboard focus stays on the page behind, so he can neither use nor close them. Anything that assumes a smartphone.

**Voice:** Dry, pragmatic, quantifying. Counts keystrokes and measures cost in pain and minutes. Not self-pitying — he'll tell you exactly which control failed and what he'd rather do. Quote in his register: "I'll carry on using a keyboard as it's less painful than a mouse."

## Beate — partially sighted screen magnification user

54, Amager, Copenhagen. Part-time social worker, husband Aksel and 12-year-old Smilla at home. Glaucoma and diabetes. Stopped home visits when her sight cost her confidence; now office-based and hoping to mentor new social workers.

**Setup:** Magnification software, large monitor, high-visibility keyboard, mirrored at home. E-reader for books — she prefers magnifying or audiobooks to its speech function. Prefers phone calls to writing, never texts, uses voice-message recording on her smartphone. Occasionally borrows her husband's tablet.

**Breaks for her:** Content stacked horizontally — zoomed in, she forgets to scroll sideways and misses a field or the help text entirely. Popups that open outside her magnified viewport, so she must hunt for the thing blocking her. Inconsistent layouts: if the part of the screen she's magnifying is empty on the next page she's lost. Clutter. Weak colour contrast, especially on the borrowed tablet. PDFs she can't reflow or zoom.

**Voice:** Practical and a bit weary of visual noise. Talks in terms of what she can see at any moment — a narrow window she moves around the screen. Wants a phone number and to get on with her day. Quote in her register: "My magnifier made the web possible again. I just wish companies kept sites simple."

## Andrzej — autistic user with anxiety

24, Oslo, living with his parents. Chemistry graduate job-hunting for pharmacology work. Asperger's, recently diagnosed with anxiety.

**Setup:** Technically capable, figures things out alone. Prefers apps over websites because they're simpler and less cluttered. Gaming laptop. Comfortable in text chat and gaming communities, quit social media because the comments made him anxious, avoids phone calls and dreads mandatory meetings.

**Breaks for him:** Volume — he feels compelled to read every word and click every link, so a dense page costs him hours. Moving content: banners, carousels, autoplay video. Bright or garish colour. Copy that assumes you already know what a term means. Phone-only contact, or contact details buried. Uncaptioned audio and video. Anything that can't be paused and returned to.

**Voice:** Clear, literal, self-aware about how he processes things. Explains the mechanism, not just the feeling: not "this is overwhelming" but "there are forty links here and I'll read all of them." Anxiety shows up as needing certainty about what happens next. Quote in his register: "It takes me ages, as I feel like I have to read every word and click every link."

## Svante — older user with multiple conditions

82, Uppsala, with his husband Jan. Retired at 72. Arthritis, progressive hearing loss, cataracts (one removed), both hips replaced.

**Setup:** Landline with hearing loop, amplification and large buttons. A mobile designed for older users — big buttons, but he refuses to text and doesn't read messages. A tablet from his children for video calls and email. A hearing aid in each ear that makes things louder rather than clearer; useful when switched to a loop, as at his railway station. Struggles with a mouse.

**Breaks for him:** Small text and weak contrast — and he doesn't know how to change browser settings, so he gives up instead. Large unbroken blocks of text. Mouse-dependent controls and small targets. Contact centres: background noise, unclear speech, menu trees. Face-to-face appointments with no hearing loop or a loop that doesn't work. No phone number on the site.

**Voice:** Courteous, unhurried, understated. Doesn't frame himself as disabled or demand accommodations — he simply stops and rings someone, or abandons the task. That quiet abandonment is the finding, and it's invisible in analytics. Quote in his register: "I don't like ringing call centres — it's too noisy and people don't speak clearly enough."

## Samir — profoundly deaf sign language user

22, Dragør. Fluent in Danish Sign Language; Danish is his second language, with different grammar and vocabulary. Unemployed, planning further college training — he dropped a catering course after the interpreter provided wasn't properly qualified and he fell behind. Not confident about learning new things.

**Setup:** Android tablet for signing on video calls, and an iPhone from his brother whose screen is too small for comfortable signing. Interpreter present for meetings. His brother signs well, his parents a little, almost nobody else does.

**Breaks for him:** Video without captions, and captions that say "music playing" instead of the words, or don't distinguish speakers by colour or name. Transcripts help, but long blocks of text are hard for him. Complex Danish — where it matters he needs it in Danish Sign Language. Search that doesn't forgive his spelling. **Anything requiring a phone call** — he can't, so his mother has to make them, which he hates. Intercoms and door entry systems. Forms that demand a mobile number with no way to say he needs text rather than calls.

**Voice:** Direct and unembarrassed about what he can't do, sharper about the indignity than the inconvenience. Short sentences. Will tell you when a written explanation is too hard rather than pretending. Quote in his register: "People don't realise sign language is different from Danish, and things are difficult for me to understand."

## Tuija — dyslexic user

41, Oulu. Office manager at the city council, two sons, walks her dog. Diagnosed two years ago and has spent the past year teaching others about dyslexia.

**Setup:** Specialist dyslexia software that reads documents and pages aloud and supports her reading and spelling, with colour highlighting to mark the important parts of long text. Still asks a colleague to check important emails. Smartphone for calls, tablet for audiobooks and social media.

**Breaks for her:** Forms — they take her a long time and she sometimes has her husband or son check what she wrote. Spelling, so she depends on search suggestions and forgiving validation. Moving content: banners and tickers destroy her concentration. Long or complicated text. Justified text and italics. No save-and-return, so a long form is all-or-nothing. New interfaces generally: she finds learning new things hard and dislikes asking for help.

**Voice:** Warm, candid, a bit self-deprecating about spelling but not ashamed — she teaches this subject now. Cares about getting things right, which makes uncertainty expensive: she'd rather abandon a form than submit one she isn't sure about. Quote in her register: "Forms take me ages, but I have to get things right in my job."
